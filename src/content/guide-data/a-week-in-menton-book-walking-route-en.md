# A Week in Menton: Walk Through the Places Behind the Story

### A week by the sea. A story about everything that wasn't planned.

Some people arrive in Menton with a suitcase.

Alex arrives with a schedule.

He's thirty-eight, works in exhibition projects and has become rather good at making complicated things run according to plan. His holiday is supposed to be different. A whole week on the French Riviera, carefully organised to make the most of every day.

There are places to visit, restaurants to investigate and walks already marked on his map.

Then he opens the balcony door.

And the Mediterranean, quite unhelpfully, refuses to follow the itinerary.

_A Week in Menton: A Novella by the Sea_ is a new work of fiction by **Anton Biletskyi-Volokh**, published in October 2026 in partnership with AzurMenton.

Set in early autumn, the novella follows Alex through seven days of small discoveries, unexpected conversations, local food, changed plans and ordinary moments that begin to matter more than he anticipated.

It's a story about travelling, but also about the strange difficulty of allowing yourself to do nothing in particular.

And Menton isn't merely a decorative background.

Its streets, market, promenade, old town and Mediterranean views are essential to the atmosphere of the book.

The characters and their encounters are fictional. The places are real.

Which means you can explore them yourself.

## The literary walk: follow the real Menton behind the fiction

This isn't a scene-by-scene reconstruction of the novel.

Alex's week doesn't unfold as one continuous sightseeing route, and the fictional café and apartment have no real-world addresses.

Instead, we've connected the actual places identified in the book into a practical, enjoyable walk.

## Stop 1. Promenade du Soleil: start by doing less

**The setting:** The seafront promenade of Menton.

There's a particular kind of arrival that happens when you've reached your destination physically but your mind is still travelling.

You're checking messages, looking at the time, wondering what needs to happen next.

Alex knows the feeling.

His holiday begins with a plan, a small apartment and a balcony overlooking the sea. But the waterfront gradually offers him something that doesn't fit neatly into his schedule: permission to stop.

Promenade du Soleil is a good place to begin your own visit for exactly the same reason.

The promenade follows the Mediterranean, passing palms, Belle Époque buildings, cafés and long stretches of open water.

You can walk towards the old town, watch the changing colours of the sea or sit with a coffee.

There isn't a particular attraction you need to reach.

**Try this:** Spend ten minutes looking at the sea without taking a photograph or checking your phone.

If that sounds easy, you may be better at holidays than Alex.

**Practical notes:** The promenade is freely accessible. It's exposed to wind, particularly outside summer, and café opening hours vary by season.

## Stop 2. Rue Saint-Michel: the pleasure of an ordinary breakfast

**The setting:** Menton's pedestrian shopping street and lower old town.

One of the most appealing parts of _A Week in Menton_ is its attention to small everyday rituals.

Breakfast becomes more than something to get out of the way before sightseeing.

A familiar table, a cup of coffee, a croissant and a few ordinary conversations become part of the experience of being somewhere new.

Rue Saint-Michel is one of the real locations connected with this part of the story.

It's a pedestrian street lined with shops, cafés and colourful buildings, and it leads towards the historic centre.

You can buy something from a bakery, stop for coffee or simply explore the shopfronts.

Rather than looking for an exact café from the novel, choose one that appeals to you.

**A note for readers:** Nadia and the café associated with Alex's breakfasts are fictional. No existing business is being represented as their real-world counterpart.

**Try this:** Order breakfast without researching five alternatives first. Then sit long enough to enjoy it.

## Stop 3. Marché des Halles: lemons and the art of asking

**The setting:** Marché des Halles, Quai de Monléon.

Menton is famous for its lemons.

For many visitors, that means the spectacular sculptures of February's Lemon Festival or the colourful citrus products in local shops.

But lemons are also part of ordinary life here.

At the covered municipal market, fruit and vegetables sit beside cheeses, bread, local products and ingredients for everyday cooking.

In the novella, Alex buys lemons and asks Élise how to use them.

It's a small scene that captures something central to the book: the difference between knowing how to organise something and being willing to ask somebody else.

The real Marché des Halles is an ideal place to experience that side of Menton.

### What to look for

Seasonal citrus fruit, local produce, regional cheeses, olives and ingredients for a simple Mediterranean meal.

The market's historic building also makes the visit worthwhile even if you're not planning to cook.

**Try this:** Buy one unfamiliar ingredient and ask the seller how they would prepare it.

You may leave with something more interesting than a souvenir.

**Practical notes:** The municipality publishes ordinary market hours of approximately 7:30 a.m. to 1 p.m. daily. Individual stalls and holiday arrangements may vary. Plan this stop for the morning if you want to see the market in operation.

## Stop 4. Saint-Michel Basilica: a view worth changing your plans for

**The setting:** Parvis Saint-Michel, above the old town.

From the lower streets of Menton, the basilica seems to watch over everything.

Its baroque façade and bell tower rise above terracotta roofs, narrow lanes and the sea.

Reaching the square involves climbing through the historic town, passing stone steps, colourful buildings and small architectural details.

Alex considers this climb earlier in his stay before finally reaching the square on Sunday.

It's a useful reminder that not every worthwhile destination needs to be reached immediately.

### What makes the square special?

The forecourt of Saint-Michel Basilica is one of Menton's most memorable architectural spaces.

The pebble paving, historic buildings and adjoining Chapel of the White Penitents create a distinctly theatrical setting.

And then there's the view.

The old port and Les Sablettes lie below, with the Mediterranean beyond.

It's one of those places where turning around to look at what you've just climbed past can be as rewarding as reaching the top.

**Try this:** Sit or stand quietly for a few minutes and watch the town below.

Notice the contrast between the narrow historic streets and the openness of the sea.

**Practical notes:** Access to the square involves substantial stairs and gradients. The basilica's interior has its own opening hours and religious services, so don't assume it's open whenever you reach the forecourt.

## Stop 5. Les Sablettes: see the town from below

**The setting:** Plage des Sablettes and Esplanade des Sablettes.

After climbing into Menton's old town, it's worth seeing the same landscape from the opposite perspective.

Les Sablettes lies beneath the basilica, beside the old port.

From the waterfront, the colourful buildings rise in layers above the beach, creating one of the most recognisable views of the French Riviera.

The contrast is particularly beautiful in early autumn, when the sea may still be warm and the strongest summer crowds have begun to fade.

In the novella, Les Sablettes forms part of the real landscape connected with Alex's Sunday walk.

For visitors, it offers something more straightforward: the possibility of slowing down beside the water.

### What to do

Walk along the esplanade.

Find a place to sit.

Watch the boats, the changing light and the buildings above the beach.

Have an ice cream if the shops are open.

Or do absolutely nothing for a little while.

It's a perfectly respectable holiday activity.

**Practical notes:** The public waterfront is accessible throughout the year under normal conditions. Beach facilities and commercial services may be seasonal. If descending from the basilica, choose your route according to your mobility and comfort with stairs.

## Stop 6. Vieux Château cemetery: the view you don't have to reach

**The setting:** The historic hilltop cemetery above Menton's old town.

Some places are particularly interesting because of what we decide not to do.

High above Saint-Michel Basilica lies the Vieux Château cemetery, built on the site of Menton's former castle.

It's known for its remarkable views over the town, the Mediterranean and the Italian coastline.

It's also home to historic graves associated with the international residents who came to Menton during its years as a fashionable winter resort.

Alex considers continuing farther uphill but leaves the cemetery for a future visit.

That makes it an appropriate final stop for this literary walk.

You can climb up and enjoy one of the town's most dramatic viewpoints.

Or you can decide that you've walked enough, return to the waterfront and save it for next time.

Both choices fit the spirit of the book.

**Try this:** If you reach the viewpoint, look back across the places you've visited.

Can you recognise the market, the old town or the curve of the promenade?

**Practical notes:** The climb involves additional stairs and steep lanes. Check current cemetery access hours and behave respectfully, as this is an active place of remembrance rather than simply a viewpoint.


## Bonus chapter: an unexpected day in Villefranche-sur-Mer

The novella doesn't take place entirely in Menton.

One of its real-world settings is Villefranche-sur-Mer, a beautiful coastal town between Nice and Monaco.

In the story, Alex leaves a train journey intended for Nice and spends an unplanned day in Villefranche.

Without revealing why, it's fair to say that this change of destination reflects one of the book's recurring ideas: sometimes the most memorable part of a trip begins when the original plan stops being important.

### Why visit Villefranche-sur-Mer?

The town sits beside a deep natural bay, with colourful waterfront buildings, narrow streets and a very different atmosphere from Menton.

It's a wonderful place for a relaxed half-day excursion.

### A simple Villefranche itinerary

Arrive by regional train, after checking your actual service.

Explore the old streets and descend towards the waterfront.

Spend time beside the bay.

Choose somewhere for lunch without trying to fit several attractions into the same afternoon.

Return to Menton when you're ready, using a confirmed train.

**A note for readers:** Elena and the lunch encounter described in the novel are fictional. This is an opportunity to experience the real town, not to locate an invented restaurant.

### How much time should you allow?

Approximately half a day, including transport, is a comfortable starting point.

You can stay longer if you want to explore the citadel, beaches or surrounding streets.

Check opening times for individual attractions before travelling.


## Three ways to enjoy this literary route

### The two-hour introduction

Best for visitors with limited time.

Walk along Promenade du Soleil, explore Rue Saint-Michel, visit the market if open and finish at Les Sablettes.

You can appreciate the seaside atmosphere and daily rhythm that shape the book without tackling the steepest part of Menton's historic centre.

### The complete Menton walk

Best for readers and visitors comfortable with stairs.

Follow all six stops, allowing time for the basilica square and optional climb towards the cemetery.

Don't rush.

A coffee stop, a market purchase or an unplanned pause beside the sea is part of the experience.

### The literary weekend

Best for visitors staying two or three nights.

**Day one:** Settle in, enjoy the promenade and explore the old town.

**Day two:** Visit the market, basilica and Les Sablettes, taking time for a relaxed lunch.

**Day three:** If transport and weather permit, make the separate trip to Villefranche-sur-Mer.

This version gives you space to experience the places without turning the book into a sightseeing checklist.

## What is real, and what belongs to the novel?

_A Week in Menton_ is fiction.

Alex, Claire, Nadia, Élise, Elena and the other characters are invented.

So are the cafés, shops, encounters and guest accommodation described in the narrative.

The geographical setting is real, and the book's own _Places Behind the Story_ identifies the locations used in this guide.

This distinction matters.

The aim isn't to claim that a particular café was the place where Alex had breakfast or that a certain apartment inspired his stay.

It's to explore the real city through the atmosphere and questions raised by a fictional story.

You can walk the same streets without trying to reproduce the same week.

## Why Menton makes such a good setting for a story

There's something unusual about Menton as a literary destination.

It's small enough to become familiar over a week, but varied enough that each day can feel different.

Within a relatively compact area, you can find a historic market, baroque architecture, seaside promenades, quiet cafés, steep old-town passages and views towards another country.

You can take a train to Italy or Nice.

Or you can stay exactly where you are.

That flexibility is what makes Menton such an effective setting for _A Week in Menton_.

The story isn't built around a sequence of famous attractions.

It's about what happens between them.

A breakfast that takes longer than expected.

A conversation you hadn't arranged.

An ordinary purchase that becomes a memory.

A destination you reach later than planned.

Or a day when the best thing to do turns out to be less than you'd intended.

You don't need to read the novella to appreciate these experiences.

But after reading it, you may notice them differently.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is A Week in Menton about?

It's a contemporary novella about Alex, a thirty-eight-year-old professional who arrives in Menton for a carefully planned holiday. The story follows a week of everyday discoveries, unexpected encounters and changing priorities. This description doesn't reveal the plot's later developments.

### Is the book a travel guide?

No. It's a work of fiction set in real locations. This companion article provides a practical route through some of the real places mentioned in the story.

### Are the characters based on real Menton residents?

The book identifies its characters, businesses and encounters as fictional. No association with real local establishments is implied.

### Do I need to read the book before taking the walk?

Not at all. The route works as a self-guided introduction to Menton's seafront, market and historic centre.

### Does the guide contain spoilers?

No. It discusses the setting and general atmosphere without explaining the story's important developments or ending.

### How long does the walk take?

Allow approximately two to three hours for the main Menton route, including breaks. The cemetery is an optional uphill extension. Villefranche-sur-Mer is a separate half-day trip.

### Can I do the walk without climbing stairs?

You can enjoy the promenade, lower shopping streets, market and Sablettes waterfront without following the steep basilica and cemetery extensions. The complete historic route is not step-free.

### Is the fictional apartment available to rent?

The apartment in the novella is invented. Real accommodation options in Menton should be considered separately, according to their location, facilities and availability.

## Read the story, then discover your own Menton

Perhaps you'll follow every stop in this guide.

Perhaps you'll start at the market and never reach the cemetery.

You might spend an afternoon in Villefranche-sur-Mer, find a café you love or discover a part of Menton that doesn't appear in the novel at all.

That's the point.

_A Week in Menton_ is a story about an arranged holiday that becomes something less predictable.

Its real locations offer a way to explore the city, but they aren't instructions for having the same experience as its characters.

Your own week will be different.

And that's exactly how it should be.
